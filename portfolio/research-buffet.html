Design
Menu-order experiment

Does menu order change what people choose?

Participants saw menus with food options or price tiers in different orders. The studies compared how much people planned to eat and whether they chose a higher-priced option.

Lower planned intake came with fewer premium choices

Putting healthier options first reduced planned calorie intake in both studies. In Study 2, the share choosing a higher-priced tier fell from 52.4% to 23.8%.

What a restaurant could test

Try changing menu order while watching both food choices and interest in higher-priced tiers. A design that supports one goal may work against another.

These were stated choices and planned intake. The studies did not measure food eaten or waste in an operating restaurant.

How it was studied

Change the order

Show food options or price tiers in different orders.

Compare the choices

An ordinal logit model examines the choice of higher-priced tiers in Study 2.

Examine perceived value

The analysis examines whether people’s sense of value helps explain the choice. The reported indirect effect is −0.339, CI [−0.56, −0.15].

Statistical detail

Menu condition → perceived value → tier choice

The mediation model helps interpret the trade-off. The odds ratio of 0.52 means lower odds, not a 48 percentage-point fall in probability.

Proposed next study

Move from intended choice to restaurant behavior

Test the menu intervention where diners order and eat.

Question
Does healthier-first ordering change actual intake or waste without an unacceptable commercial trade-off?
Design & unit
Field experiment with the randomization unit fixed before recruitment.
Measures & analysis
Observe consumption or waste and spending; estimate contrasts with dependence at the assignment unit.
Data & sample
Requires restaurant cooperation, consistent measurement and participant information. Plan sample size from a meaningful primary effect, clustering and implementation loss.
Decision gate
Set the acceptable commercial trade-off before the trial. Do not substitute intended calories for observed consumption.

Study notes & sources

Study design
Study 1: buffet/hotpot choice experiment, n = 257. Study 2: price-tier choice experiment, n = 147. The source describes a 2 × 3 between-subjects design and ordinal logistic analysis.
Tier choice
Higher-tier odds ratio: 0.52, meaning 48% lower odds, not a 48-point decrease in probability. Premium choice share changes from 52.4% to 23.8%.
Mediation
Reported indirect effect through perceived value: −0.339, 95% CI [−0.56, −0.15]. This helps interpret the commercial trade-off in the experimental conditions.
